Château de Versailles

Sat Jun 20 • 11:00 AM • Passeport, Entrance A
The Big Picture Versailles is the trip's "who owns Paris?" question made enormous. The Sun King built the biggest political stage set in Europe to show that he was the state — and a century later a revolution dragged the royal family back to Paris and turned palaces into the people's museums. For Levi, the royal-history fan, this is the main event: the place where French royal power peaked, and where it began to end.

📖Before You Go — The Story

It started small: in 1623 Louis XIII built a modest hunting lodge here. His son, Louis XIV — the "Sun King" — spent decades (1661–1715) inflating it into the largest palace in Europe, and in 1682 moved the entire court and government out to Versailles. Keeping hundreds of nobles dancing attendance at the palace was a clever way to control them.

The showstopper is the Hall of Mirrors: 73 m long, lined with 357 mirrors — and in the 1680s, mirrors were cutting-edge luxury tech (Venice guarded the secret), so a whole hallway of them was like paving a corridor with flat-screen TVs. Centuries later, the treaty ending World War I was signed in this very room (1919).

Then the script flipped. In October 1789 the Women's March on Versailles forced Louis XVI back to Paris, and the palace's days as the seat of power were over.

Lucky timing: Jun 20 is a Saturday, so the baroque Musical Fountains Show should be running — hidden waterworks "dancing" to music written for Louis XIV — and your Passeport covers it.

🕵️For Levi & Micah — Spot These!

  • Count the sun faces on gates, fountains and ceilings — the Sun King's personal logo.
  • The Hall of Mirrors: 357 mirrors, back when mirrors were as fancy as a wall of TVs.
  • The fountains are baroque special effects — there wasn't even enough water to run them all at once.
  • It began as a teenager's hunting cabin — then became the biggest flex in Europe.
  • Myth-buster: Marie-Antoinette almost certainly never said "Let them eat cake" — the line is older than her.
