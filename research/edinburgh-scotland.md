## Regional arc

Everything the Sanders family will see in Scotland — castle, underground street, mountain hike, misty glen, even the monster — starts with the same plot device: **fire, then ice**. Around 340–350 million years ago, this corner of Scotland was a field of active volcanoes. Two of their dead cores still dominate Edinburgh: **Castle Rock** and **Arthur's Seat**, whose lavas have been dated to roughly 341–335 million years old ([Wikipedia: Arthur's Seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur's_Seat)). Ice Age glaciers later scraped past the hard volcanic plugs, carving the "crag and tail" landform: a sheer-sided rock with a long sloping ridge trailing behind it. That tail is the **Royal Mile** — the entire medieval city was essentially one street running down a glacier's wake, from fortress (Castle Rock) to abbey and palace (Holyrood). The street really is about a mile long, but a *Scots* mile — roughly 1.81 km, longer than the family's Virginia mile ([Wikipedia: Royal Mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mile)).

Geography wrote the next chapter too. A city squeezed onto one defensible ridge, hemmed in by walls, couldn't sprawl outward — so it grew **up** (tenement "lands" of remarkable height) and **down**, into the steep alleys called **closes** that drop away from the Royal Mile on both sides. Rich and poor stacked on top of each other; sanitation was medieval; and when plague arrived in **1645** it tore through the overcrowded closes, killing what sources estimate as a quarter to a third of the city's population ([History Skills: Mary King's Close](https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/year-9/mary-king-s-close/)). **Mary King's Close** — quarantined during that outbreak, then partially demolished and built over in the 1750s as foundations for the Royal Exchange (today's City Chambers) — survives as a frozen slice of that vertical, crowded, smelly, story-rich city. The family will literally walk *underneath* the chapter of history they strolled through that afternoon.

Up on the ridge, the **crown** thread runs through everything (this is Levi's storyline). St Giles' was founded in **1124** by King David I ([St Giles' Cathedral: History](https://www.stgilescathedral.org.uk/history)). Mary, Queen of Scots gave birth to **James VI in Edinburgh Castle in 1566**; in **1603** that baby united the crowns of Scotland and England ([Royal.uk: James VI and I](https://www.royal.uk/james-vi-and-i-r-1567-1625)). In the castle's Crown Room sit the **Honours of Scotland** — crown, sceptre, sword — the oldest surviving crown jewels in the British Isles ([Historic UK](https://www.historic-uk.com/HistoryUK/HistoryofScotland/The-Honours-of-Scotland/)). After the 1707 Union of Parliaments they were locked in a chest and *forgotten for 111 years* until novelist **Walter Scott** had the Crown Room opened on 4 February 1818 and found them wrapped in linen, exactly as left ([National Library of Scotland](https://digital.nls.uk/scotlandspages/timeline/1818.html)). Religion kept colliding with the crown along the way: John Knox preached the Reformation from St Giles'; in **1637** a market trader named Jenny Geddes allegedly hurled her stool at the Dean for reading the King's new prayer book, sparking riots that helped ignite the Wars of the Three Kingdoms — though historians note she may be more legend than person ([Wikipedia: Jenny Geddes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenny_Geddes)).

The Highlands tell the same crown story from the other side. When William III replaced the exiled James II/VII in 1689, Highland clans loyal to James (the **Jacobites**) were ordered to swear allegiance by 1 January 1692. The MacDonald chief of Glencoe arrived days late through no real fault of his own — and the government made an example of him. Soldiers billeted as **guests** in MacDonald homes for nearly two weeks turned on their hosts at 5 a.m. on **13 February 1692**, killing 38 — "murder under trust," planned in Edinburgh and London ([National Trust for Scotland](https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/glencoe/the-glencoe-massacre); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Massacre-of-Glencoe)). Glen Coe's brooding beauty and that betrayal are now inseparable.

Then Edinburgh's genius third act: the **Enlightenment**. While Highland clan society was being dismantled, Edinburgh became "the Athens of the North." **James Hutton** stared at the volcanic rock of Salisbury Crags — beside the path the family will hike — and realized Earth must be unimaginably old, founding modern geology ([Wikipedia: James Hutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hutton)). The city even tried to build its own Parthenon on **Calton Hill** (begun 1826, abandoned unfinished in 1829 when money ran out — forever "Edinburgh's Disgrace," [Wikipedia: National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Monument_of_Scotland)). And Walter Scott, finder of the Honours, re-branded the defeated Highlands as the romantic soul of Scotland — a myth-making machine that runs straight through to **Nessie**, who surfaced in the newspapers in 1933 and whose most famous portrait, the 1934 "surgeon's photograph," was confessed in 1994 to be a toy submarine with a sculpted neck ([Britannica: Loch Ness monster](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Loch-Ness-monster-legendary-creature)).

**Recurring themes for the stop pages:**
1. **The rock writes the story.** Volcanoes → fortress → one-street city → overcrowding → underground city → Hutton reading deep time in the same stone. Geology is the quiet main character.
2. **Crowns, oaths, and broken trust.** (Levi's thread.) Honours of Scotland, baby James VI, Jacobites, Glencoe's "murder under trust" — Scottish history keeps turning on who swore what to whom.
3. **Build up, dig down — a city in layers.** Tenements above, closes below; every stop literally stands on an older one.
4. **Scotland the storyteller: legend vs. evidence.** Jenny Geddes, ghost Annie, Nessie — make the kids "evidence detectives" who get to rule on each tale. (Ted Lasso tie-in for the Loch Ness page: it's all about whether you choose to BELIEVE.)

## Per-stop notes

### Stop 1 — Arrival wander: Royal Mile, St Giles' Cathedral, Calton Hill (Jun 11)

**Verified facts**
- The Royal Mile runs downhill Castle → Holyrood, comprising Castlehill, the Lawnmarket, the High Street, the Canongate and Abbey Strand; it's about one *Scots* mile (~1.81 km / 1,976 yards vs. the standard 1,760). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mile
- St Giles' was founded in **1124** under King David I; its landmark **crown spire** tower dates to the 15th century (dendrochronology places the crown steeple's timber at 1460–1467; rebuilt by John Mylne in 1648). John Knox preached here and was elected its minister during the Reformation. Sources: https://www.stgilescathedral.org.uk/history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Giles'_Cathedral
- **23 July 1637**: Jenny Geddes is said to have thrown her stool at Dean James Hannay at the first reading of the new prayer book, starting a riot — but historians caution she may not have existed as described. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenny_Geddes
- The **Heart of Midlothian** mosaic outside St Giles' (laid 1860) marks the door of the Old Tolbooth prison, demolished 1817; locals spit on it for luck. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_of_Midlothian_(Royal_Mile)
- John Knox (d. 1572) lies under what is now **parking space 23** in Parliament Square, beside St Giles' — the old kirkyard was built and paved over. Source: https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/john-knoxs-grave
- Calton Hill's **National Monument** (Napoleonic Wars memorial modeled on the Parthenon, by C.R. Cockerell & W.H. Playfair) was begun in **1826** and abandoned unfinished in **1829** when funds ran out — nicknamed "Edinburgh's Disgrace." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Monument_of_Scotland
- The **Nelson Monument** (1807) is shaped like an upturned telescope; its **time ball** (installed 1852) drops at 1 p.m., synchronized with the Castle's One O'Clock Gun, originally so ships at Leith could set their chronometers. Sources: https://www.edinburghoutdoors.org.uk/monuments/monuments-calton-hill ; https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/edinburgh/caltonhill/index.html
- The Old and New Towns of Edinburgh were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in **1995**. Source: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/728/

**Kid hooks**
- "Gardyloo!" — in the crammed Old Town, waste went out of high windows; the closes the family's apartment sits among were once the smelliest streets in Europe. (General Old Town history; treat as color, not a dated statistic.)
- Spit on the Heart of Midlothian like a local — it marks a demolished prison door, and spitting started as an insult to the jail (verified above).
- A famous national hero is buried under a parking space (No. 23) — find the little plaque (verified above).
- Scotland built a Parthenon and ran out of money after 12 columns — the kids can judge: disgrace or masterpiece?

**Myths / mistakes**
- St Giles' is called a cathedral but has spent most of its life as a Presbyterian High Kirk, not a bishop's seat — "cathedral" stuck from two brief 17th-century episodes.
- Jenny Geddes' stool-throw is iconic but unverifiable — present it as legend with a wink, not fact.
- The "Royal Mile" name is a relatively modern label for a string of separately named streets — don't look for one street sign that says "Royal Mile."

**Practical**
- Jun 11 arrival: Calton Hill is the low-effort, high-reward sunset spot — and in mid-June, Edinburgh's northern latitude means usable daylight to around 10 p.m., so it can come *after* dinner.
- St Giles' is free-entry/donation in normal practice but check current hours for evening access; the Thistle Chapel (1911, home chapel of the Order of the Thistle) is the detail most visitors miss — look for the angel playing bagpipes in its carvings (a famous carving; have the kids hunt for it).
- People walk the Mile and skip the closes — the magic is one step off the main street, which the family already lives in (Campbell's Close).

### Stop 2 — The Real Mary King's Close, 5:00 PM Jun 11

**Verified facts**
- Mary King was a real widowed merchant burgess and fabric trader who lived on the close in the 1630s after her husband's death; the close (earlier Alexander King's Close) took her name. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_King's_Close ; https://www.ontheluce.com/underground-edinburgh-mary-kings-close/
- Plague struck Edinburgh in **1645**, spreading fastest through the overcrowded closes; estimates run to a quarter–a third of the city's population dead. Source: https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/year-9/mary-king-s-close/
- In the 1750s the upper sections of the close were demolished/truncated and the remains used as **foundations for the Royal Exchange**, now the City Chambers — which is why the street survives underground. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_King's_Close
- The site reopened to the public as **The Real Mary King's Close in 2003**. Source: https://travelingboy.com/travel/mary-kings-close-edinburgh-scotland-400-years-of-history-previously-buried-literally-brought-alive/
- "Annie's Room": in **1992** visiting Japanese medium Aiko Gibo claimed to sense a plague-child ghost who'd lost her doll; visitors have left a mound of dolls and toys ever since. Sources: https://www.realmarykingsclose.com/experience/the-story-of-annies-doll/ ; https://www.ontheluce.com/underground-edinburgh-mary-kings-close/

**Kid hooks**
- A real 400-year-old street is hiding under the city council's building — and the family walks down into it.
- Plague doctors visited the close in head-to-toe leather with beaked masks stuffed with herbs (the close's plague-doctor story features Dr. George Rae, Edinburgh's plague doctor — the costume alone is worth the ticket).
- Annie's Room: a creepy, ever-growing pile of dolls left for a ghost. Micah and Levi can decide — touching tribute or nightmare fuel?
- The quarantined sick had food and water brought to them by the town — 1645's version of contactless delivery.

**Myths / mistakes**
- **The big one:** the story that plague victims were *bricked up alive* in the close is an urban myth, debunked — quarantine was harsh but the city fed its sick, and the close was emptied before being built over. Sources above (History Skills; Wandering Crystal: https://www.wanderingcrystal.com/marykingsclose/)
- It's not a "secret tunnel city" — it's the lower stories of ordinary streets entombed by later construction.

**Practical**
- 5:00 PM on arrival day is smart — it's indoors, all-weather, and guided (about an hour), perfect for jet-lagged legs. Arrive ~15 minutes early at the City Chambers entrance on the High Street, two minutes from the apartment.
- It's atmospheric-dark, not jump-scare horror — fine for the kids, but brief them that it's a costumed-guide history tour, not a haunted house.
- Photography is generally restricted inside (confirm at check-in — could not verify current policy directly).

### Stop 3 — Ideas day: Arthur's Seat + St Anthony's Chapel, Edinburgh Castle, Scotch Whisky Experience, Grassmarket (Jun 12)

**Verified facts**
- **Arthur's Seat** rises 250.5 m (822 ft); it's the eroded remains of a volcano whose lavas date to ~341–335 million years ago (early Carboniferous). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur's_Seat
- In **1836**, boys hunting rabbits on Arthur's Seat found **17 miniature coffins**, each with a tiny dressed wooden figure; 8 survive in the National Museum of Scotland; purpose still unsolved (theories include Burke & Hare's 1828 murder victims). Sources: https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/the-coffins-found-on-arthurs-seat ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/edinburghs-mysterious-miniature-coffins-22371426/
- **St Anthony's Chapel** ruins, above St Margaret's Loch in Holyrood Park: built by the very early 15th century (Pope Martin V funded repairs in **1426**); once had a ~40-ft tower; a pilgrimage site linked to cures for "St Anthony's Fire" (erysipelas); ruinous since roughly the post-Reformation era. Sources: https://www.edinburghnews.scotsman.com/heritage-and-retro/retro/st-anthonys-chapel-what-we-know-about-edinburghs-mysterious-ruin-3237962 ; https://canmore.org.uk/site/52457/edinburgh-holyrood-park-queens-drive-st-anthonys-chapel-and-hermitage
- **Hutton's Section** on Salisbury Crags is where James Hutton found volcanic rock intruding through sediments — keystone evidence for modern geology. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hutton
- **Edinburgh Castle**: research in 2014 counted **26 sieges** in ~1,100 years — a claim to "most besieged place in Great Britain." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_Castle
- **St Margaret's Chapel** (c. 1130, built under David I for his mother) is Edinburgh's oldest building — spared even when Robert the Bruce had the rest of the castle's buildings destroyed in 1314. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Margaret's_Chapel,_Edinburgh ; https://www.edinburghcastle.scot/see-and-do/highlights/st-margarets-chapel/
- The **Honours of Scotland** (crown remodeled for James V, first worn 1540; sceptre; sword) are the oldest crown jewels in the British Isles; sealed away after the 1707 Union and rediscovered by Walter Scott's commission on **4 Feb 1818**; on display since 26 May 1819. Sources: https://www.historic-uk.com/HistoryUK/HistoryofScotland/The-Honours-of-Scotland/ ; https://digital.nls.uk/scotlandspages/timeline/1818.html
- **Mons Meg**, the giant siege gun, was given to James II in **1457**; in 1460 oxen could haul her barely 3 miles a day. Source: https://www.edinburghcastle.scot/see-and-do/highlights/mons-meg/
- The **One O'Clock Gun** has fired since **1861** (idea imported from Paris by businessman John Hewat) — daily at 1 p.m. except Sundays, Good Friday and Christmas Day. Sources: https://www.edinburghcastle.scot/see-and-do/highlights/one-oclock-gun/ ; https://blog.edinburghcastle.scot/the-origins-of-the-one-oclock-gun/
- **James VI** was born in the castle's Royal Palace on **19 June 1566** to Mary, Queen of Scots; king at 13 months; united the crowns in 1603. Source: https://www.royal.uk/james-vi-and-i-r-1567-1625
- **Scotch Whisky Experience** (Castlehill, in a Victorian former school): barrel dark-ride through whisky-making, plus the **Diageo Claive Vidiz Collection** — **3,384 bottles** amassed over ~35 years by a Brazilian collector, bought by Diageo in 2008, displayed from May 2009. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scotch_Whisky_Experience ; https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/edinburgh/whiskyexperience/index.html

**Kid hooks**
- You're climbing a real (extinct!) volcano before lunch — older than the dinosaurs by ~100 million years.
- The 17 tiny coffins: Scotland's creepiest unsolved mystery, found by kids about Levi and Micah's age. (If energy allows, the surviving 8 are FREE to see at the National Museum of Scotland.)
- The castle was attacked 26 times — record-breaker for Britain — and Mons Meg could fire a ~150 kg gunstone (have them lift an imaginary one).
- A future king was born in a tiny room here in 1566, and the crown jewels were *lost inside their own castle* for 111 years until a novelist found them in a chest.
- 1 p.m. sharp: a real artillery gun fires from the castle — watch tourists jump.

**Myths / mistakes**
- **The Stone of Destiny is no longer at Edinburgh Castle.** It left on 10 March 2024 and is now the centerpiece of Perth Museum (displayed from 30 March 2024). Don't promise it in the Crown Room — the Honours are still there and still spectacular. Sources: https://perthmuseum.co.uk/the-stone-of-destiny/ ; https://www.stonespecialist.com/news/stone-heritage/stone-destiny-arrives-its-new-home-perth-museum
- Arthur's Seat's name almost certainly isn't proof King Arthur sat there — the etymology is uncertain; treat the name as a legend layer.
- The castle isn't "ancient ruins" — it's a working military site and the buildings span 900 years; the oldest bit is the tiny chapel, not the big walls.
- Whisky Experience: kids are welcome on the standard tour (drivers and kids get a non-alcoholic alternative at the tasting; confirm child policy when booking — not directly verified here).

**Practical**
- Order of battle: hike Arthur's Seat in the morning cool (allow ~2 hrs round trip from Holyrood; the St Anthony's Chapel ruins sit just above St Margaret's Loch on the north approach — an easy add-on and superb photo stop), castle early afternoon (prebook timed entry; June is peak), whisky barrel ride late afternoon (it's 100 m from the castle esplanade), then downhill to the Grassmarket — the old market-and-executions square below the castle, now wall-to-wall family-friendly pubs with castle views.
- Be in the castle grounds just before 1:00 p.m. for the gun — or, alternate plan, hear it echo from the Royal Mile.
- Most-missed at the castle: St Margaret's Chapel (people walk past the smallest, oldest thing) and the view *of Arthur's Seat* — fun once you've climbed it.

### Stop 4 — Rabbie's full-day coach tour: Loch Ness, Glen Coe & the Highlands (Jun 13)

**Verified facts**
- Rabbie's "Loch Ness, Glencoe & the Highlands" from Edinburgh: 16-seat Mercedes mini-coach, ~12-hour day; route passes the Kelpies and Stirling Castle, crosses the Highland Boundary Fault, traverses Rannoch Moor to Glen Coe, with a lunch stop at Fort Augustus on Loch Ness and an optional ~50-minute Loch Ness cruise; departs Edinburgh Bus Station (check-in closes 07:30). Sources: https://www.rabbies.com/en-us/tour/loch-ness-glencoe-highlands-day-tour-from-edinburgh ; https://www.viator.com/tours/Edinburgh/Loch-Ness-Glencoe-and-the-Highlands-Small-Group-Day-Trip-from-Edinburgh/d739-225005
- **Massacre of Glencoe, 13 Feb 1692**: after the MacDonald chief missed the 1 Jan 1692 deadline to swear allegiance to William III (no magistrate at Fort William; sworn 6 Jan), ~120 soldiers under Robert Campbell of Glenlyon were quartered with the MacDonalds for nearly two weeks, then killed **38** of their hosts at 5 a.m. — "murder under trust," ordered through Secretary of State John Dalrymple, Master of Stair, under the king's signature. Sources: https://www.britannica.com/event/Massacre-of-Glencoe ; https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/glencoe/the-glencoe-massacre
- **Loch Ness**: deepest point ~230 m (755 ft); largest lake by volume in the British Isles — more water than all the lakes of England and Wales combined. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loch_Ness
- **Urquhart Castle** ruins stand on the loch's western shore ~2 km east of Drumnadrochit (note: most Fort Augustus cruises view the loch, not the castle — castle visits depend on the tour's options). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loch_Ness
- **Nessie**: modern phenomenon dates from **1933**; the famous 1934 "surgeon's photograph" (Daily Mail, attributed to Dr. Robert Kenneth Wilson) was exposed in **1994** when Christian Spurling admitted it was a sculpted neck on a toy submarine. Sources: https://www.britannica.com/topic/Loch-Ness-monster-legendary-creature ; https://hoaxes.org/photo_database/image/the_surgeons_photo/

**Kid hooks**
- Loch Ness holds more water than every lake in England and Wales *put together* — if Nessie wanted to hide anywhere, this is the spot.
- The most famous monster photo ever was a toy submarine with a clay neck — and the hoaxers kept the secret for 60 years.
- Glen Coe's three big peaks are "The Three Sisters"; the glen itself is the collapsed wreck of an ancient supervolcano-caldera landscape (guides love this one — let Rabbie's tell it).
- Spot the Kelpies from the coach: 30-m steel horse heads, the biggest equine sculptures in the world (passed early in the drive).
- Believe-o-meter (Ted Lasso edition): the family votes on Nessie before and after the cruise. BELIEVE.

**Myths / mistakes**
- Glencoe wasn't a simple "Campbells vs. MacDonalds clan feud" — it was a **government operation** planned in Edinburgh and London under King William's authority; Campbell of Glenlyon led the troops, which is how the clan-feud shorthand stuck. Sources above (NTS; Britannica).
- The number killed is usually given as 38 (some accounts add more who died of exposure fleeing into the winter mountains) — say "around 38" rather than an exact universal figure.
- Nessie "sightings" before 1933 (St Columba, 565 AD) are retrofitted onto a modern media phenomenon — fun to mention, weak as evidence.
- Don't expect to "do" Urquhart Castle on this itinerary unless the day's options include it — the standard lunch/cruise stop is Fort Augustus at the loch's southern end.

**Practical**
- It's a ~12-hour day (roughly 8 a.m.–8 p.m.) — pack snacks, layers and rain shells (Glen Coe makes its own weather, even in June), and download entertainment for the kids; reviewers consistently call it a long day.
- Check-in closes 07:30 at Edinburgh Bus Station, St Andrew Square — about a 15-minute walk downhill from the Royal Mile; book the Loch Ness cruise option in advance if wanted.
- Best photo stop is usually the Three Sisters viewpoint in Glen Coe; sit on the left side heading north for loch views (anecdotal; seats are first-come in a 16-seater).
