## Regional arc

Here's the secret to this trip: in five days, the Sanders family will walk through about 5,000 years
of history — and almost all of it happens within sight of a river.

Start at the oldest stop, even though it comes last on the itinerary. Around 3000 BC, Neolithic
farmers on Salisbury Plain dug a circular ditch and bank; about five centuries later, around 2500 BC,
their descendants hauled 25-ton sarsen stones some 16 miles and dragged smaller bluestones all the
way from the Preseli Hills of Wales to raise Stonehenge (English Heritage). Nobody wrote anything
down — writing hadn't reached Britain — so the monument keeps its mystery. What we know is that
people without metal tools or wheels organized a massive, multi-generational project because it
*meant* something. That impulse — build something enormous to say who we are — never leaves this
island.

The Romans arrive in AD 43 and bring the receipts. At a steaming spring in the west country they
find Celtic Britons worshipping a goddess called Sulis; rather than bulldoze her, they merge her with
their own Minerva and build the temple-and-bath complex of Aquae Sulis around AD 60–70 (Roman Baths
museum, Wikipedia). Meanwhile, at a bridgeable point on the Thames, they found Londinium — the
walled square mile that is still called "the City," where the family will eat dinner on Jun 17. Roman
London and Roman Bath teach the same lesson: conquerors who absorb local culture last longer than
ones who erase it.

When the next conquerors come — the Normans, 1066 — they build in stone to intimidate. William the
Conqueror starts the White Tower around 1078, with masons and creamy Caen stone shipped from
Normandy, deliberately looming over his new Anglo-Saxon subjects (Historic Royal Palaces, Wikipedia).
That same pivotal year, 1066, fixes Westminster Abbey as the coronation church: every crowned English
and British monarch since has been crowned there, right up to Charles III in 2023 (Wikipedia,
Westminster Abbey). So by the Middle Ages, London has its two poles: the **Tower** (raw power,
prisoners, treasure) and the **Abbey** (sacred legitimacy). The Crown Jewels Levi will see on Jun 16
have lived at the Tower since 1661; the Coronation Chair they're used with has been at the Abbey
since 1308.

Then comes the Elizabethan flowering, and history jumps the river. The City fathers thought theatres
were disreputable, so playhouses like the Globe (1599) went up on the lawless South Bank — exactly
where the family will stand as groundlings on night one. Shakespeare's London was a boomtown of
spectacle, and the Globe burned down mid-performance in 1613 when a stage cannon set the thatch
alight. The reconstruction they'll visit opened in 1997, the dream of an American actor, Sam
Wanamaker — an early hint that London's story is increasingly written by people who came from
somewhere else.

The Georgian eighteenth century turns from God and crown to elegance and leisure. Bath — the same
hot-spring town the Romans built — reinvents itself as the resort of polite society, wrapped in
honey-colored crescents by John Wood father and son (Royal Crescent, 1767–75). Jane Austen lived
there 1801–06 and set two novels in it. Bath is what Britain builds when it isn't fighting: pleasure,
architecture, gossip.

The Victorians build like the Romans on steroids. Tower Bridge (opened 1894, 11,000 tons of steel
dressed up in Gothic stone to flatter its medieval neighbor) is empire-era engineering wearing a
history costume. Borough Market — with roots in records from 1014 — gets its iron-and-glass
Victorian halls in 1851. Even Big Ben's great bell (1850s, 13.7 tonnes) is Victorian swagger
calibrated to the second.

And modern London? It's the remix. The South Bank, once the naughty theatre district, is now the
culture mile crowned by the London Eye (2000) — a Ferris wheel with exactly 32 capsules, one for
each London borough. Shoreditch, where the family sleeps, went from working-class East End to street-
art-and-coffee capital. Notting Hill went from market neighborhood to Caribbean cultural heartland to
movie-famous antiques mecca. And Richmond — leafy, riverside, deer-filled — became world-famous
again because an American TV coach named Ted Lasso was filmed walking its lanes. Sam Wanamaker
rebuilding the Globe and Ted Lasso charming Richmond are the same story, 50 years apart: Americans
falling for England, and England playing along.

**Recurring themes to thread through the tour pages:**
1. **Rivers run the show.** Thames and Avon: every stop exists because of water — bathing it,
   bridging it, crossing it for forbidden fun, cruising it on Jun 15.
2. **Invaders become ingredients.** Romans absorb Sulis; Normans build the Tower; immigrants build
   Notting Hill and Shoreditch; an American rebuilds the Globe. London digests everything.
3. **The crown is a 1,000-year stage show.** Coronations since 1066, jewels since 1661, guard
   changes at 11:00 sharp — monarchy survives by performing, and the family has front-row seats.
4. **Stone keeps the score.** From Stonehenge's sarsens to Caen stone to Bath stone to Tower
   Bridge's steel-in-stone — each era's biggest ideas are written in what it builds.

## Per-stop notes

### Stop 1 — Shakespeare's Globe: *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, standing in the Yard, 6:30 PM, Sun Jun 14

**Verified facts**
- The original Globe opened in 1599; it burned to the ground on 29 June 1613 when a cannon fired
  during *Henry VIII* ignited the thatched roof — destroyed within about an hour.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre
- The modern reconstruction, "Shakespeare's Globe," opened in 1997, about 750 ft (230 m) from the
  original site — the culmination of decades of campaigning by American actor-director Sam Wanamaker.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre
- *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, directed by Emily Lim, runs in the Globe's summer 2026 season
  (season opener; running to 29 Aug 2026). Source: https://www.shakespearesglobe.com/whats-on/a-midsummer-nights-dream/
  *(Listings sites give slightly different start dates — 23 Apr vs 1 May — so quote only "summer 2026 season.")*
- Standing Yard ("groundling") tickets are the famous cheap seats — £5 via the Globe's £5 Rush
  scheme, with standard standing tickets from ~£7; all standing is unallocated general admission.
  Source: https://www.shakespearesglobe.com/5-pound-rush/

**Kid hooks**
- The 1613 fire: a contemporary letter (Sir Henry Wotton) records that one man's breeches caught fire
  and were doused with a bottle of ale. Theatre gone in about an hour; reportedly no deaths.
- You're standing where the "groundlings" stood 400+ years ago — the rowdy crowd Shakespeare's
  actors had to win over, rain or shine (the Yard is open to the sky).
- The building is made the old way — green oak and a real thatched roof — a famous rarity in
  central London since the Great Fire of 1666 *(widely repeated claim; not independently verified here)*.

**Myths & mistakes**
- "This is Shakespeare's actual theatre." No — it's a 1997 reconstruction ~230 m from the original
  site. The original was rebuilt in 1614 and finally demolished in the 1640s.
- "Groundling tickets are bad tickets." Opposite: actors play *to* the Yard; it's the most immersive
  spot in the house — and the cheapest.

**Practical insight**
- It's ~3 hours standing; have the kids wear comfortable shoes, eat beforehand, and claim a spot
  where they can lean on the stage. No umbrellas in the Yard — rain ponchos if the sky threatens.
  *Midsummer* is the ideal first Shakespeare for kids: fairies, a man turned into a donkey, slapstick.

### Stop 3 — Westminster & South Bank: Changing of the Guard, Westminster Abbey, Big Ben, Covent Garden (Jun 15)

**Verified facts**
- Changing of the Guard at Buckingham Palace takes place Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays at 11 AM
  (plus Sundays), ceremony ~45 minutes; schedules can change at short notice — verify the date.
  Jun 15, 2026 is a Monday, so it should be on. Source: https://changing-guard.com/changing-the-guard-buckingham-palace-schedule.html
- The King's Guard is drawn from five Foot Guards regiments — bearskin caps and red tunics —
  distinguished by button groupings and plume colors. Source: https://www.householddivision.org.uk/changing-the-guard-overview
- Westminster Abbey has hosted the coronation of every English/British monarch since 1066 — 40
  monarchs, most recently Charles III in 2023; the Coronation Chair has been used since 1308.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey
- Royal graves at the Abbey: roughly 17–18 monarchs *(counts vary slightly by source)*, including
  Edward the Confessor (buried 1066 in the church he built), Henry VII, Elizabeth I, Mary I, and
  Mary Queen of Scots (moved there by her son James I in 1612). George II (d. 1760) was the last
  monarch buried there. Sources: https://www.westminster-abbey.org/history/explore-our-history/a-history-of-royal-burials-and-funerals ; https://www.unofficialroyalty.com/westminster-abbey-royal-burials/
- **For Levi — the big one:** Queen Elizabeth II is NOT buried at Westminster Abbey. She rests in the
  King George VI Memorial Chapel, a small side chapel of St George's Chapel at Windsor Castle,
  alongside Prince Philip, her parents George VI and the Queen Mother, and Princess Margaret's ashes.
  She commissioned the chapel herself in 1962 (completed 1969). Her funeral *service* was at the
  Abbey in Sept 2022 — likely the source of the confusion. Windsor is ~1 hour from central London;
  a separate Windsor Castle ticket lets you see the chapel and her ledger stone.
  Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_George_VI_Memorial_Chapel ; https://www.cbsnews.com/news/queen-elizabeth-ii-final-resting-place-photo-windsor/
- Big Ben: strictly the nickname of the Great Bell (13.7 tonnes, cast at the Whitechapel Bell
  Foundry), not the tower. The tower — originally the Clock Tower — was renamed Elizabeth Tower in
  2012 for QEII's Diamond Jubilee. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ben ; https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/building/palace/big-ben/building-clock-tower/key-dates-/

**Kid hooks**
- Elizabeth I and her half-sister "Bloody" Mary I — bitter rivals in life — share one tomb in the
  Abbey's Lady Chapel *(widely documented; inscription calls them "partners in throne and grave")*.
- The Abbey is basically a marble who's-who: Newton, Darwin, and Poets' Corner — over 3,000 people
  are buried there in total *(commonly cited figure; treat as approximate)*.
- Guards in bearskins are working soldiers, not costumed actors.

**Myths & mistakes**
- "Big Ben is the tower" — it's the bell (tower = Elizabeth Tower).
- "Queen Elizabeth II is buried at the Abbey" — she's at Windsor (see above). Frame this for Levi as
  a detective win: the family can see where she was *crowned* (1953) and where her funeral was held,
  then decide whether a Windsor side-trip fits.
- "The Changing of the Guard happens daily at Buckingham Palace" — only on scheduled days.

**Practical insight**
- The guard ceremony draws huge crowds; arrive 60–90 min early or watch from the Victoria Memorial
  steps / along The Mall for the marching bands. The Abbey needs a timed ticket and ~2 hours; one
  child (5–15 bracket) qualifies for a child ticket. Covent Garden street performers = perfect
  late-afternoon kid decompression.

### Stop 4 — Tower of London, Yeoman Warder tour, 10:00 AM, Tue Jun 16

**Verified facts**
- The White Tower was begun c. 1078 under William the Conqueror, with Gundulf, Bishop of Rochester,
  overseeing construction; Norman masons used stone shipped from Caen, France.
  Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Tower_(Tower_of_London) ; https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/history-and-stories/the-story-of-the-tower-of-london/
- Yeoman Warders ("Beefeaters") are all retired senior armed-forces personnel with at least 22 years'
  service and the Long Service and Good Conduct Medal. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeomen_Warders
- The Crown Jewels — over 100 objects set with more than 23,000 gemstones — have been kept at the
  Tower since 1661. Source: https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/ (Historic Royal Palaces)
- Anne Boleyn was executed on 19 May 1536 by an expert swordsman brought from France, and is buried
  in the Chapel Royal of St Peter ad Vincula beside Tower Green.
  Sources: https://www.hrp.org.uk/tower-of-london/history-and-stories/anne-boleyn/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Green
- At least six ravens are kept at the Tower, tended by a Yeoman Warder called the Ravenmaster.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravens_of_the_Tower_of_London

**Kid hooks**
- The raven legend: if the ravens leave, the Crown — and Britain — will fall. The birds have names,
  clipped flight feathers, and their own keeper.
- Executions on Tower Green were the "private" VIP option — including two of Henry VIII's queens
  (Anne Boleyn, Catherine Howard) and 16-year-old Lady Jane Grey, queen for about nine days.
- The Crown Jewels room has a moving walkway so gawkers can't clog the view of 23,000+ gemstones.
- Beefeaters live *inside* the fortress with their families — it's a tiny walled village.

**Myths & mistakes**
- "The raven tradition goes back to Charles II." Disputed: the Tower's own historian has called the
  raven mythology "likely a Victorian flight of fantasy" — the earliest hard evidence of captive
  ravens is from 1883. Tell both versions; the debunk is half the fun.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravens_of_the_Tower_of_London
- "It was only ever a prison." It's been a royal palace, fortress, mint, armoury, and even a royal
  menagerie (lions, a polar bear) over 950+ years.

**Practical insight**
- Yeoman Warder tours leave from near the main entrance roughly every 30 minutes and run ~1 hour;
  they're included with admission. Do the Crown Jewels immediately at opening or right after the
  tour — the queue balloons by late morning. Budget 3+ hours total; exit conveniently faces Tower
  Bridge for the afternoon plan.

### Stop 5 — South Bank Jun 16: Tower Bridge glass floor, Borough Market, London Eye at sunset

**Verified facts**
- Tower Bridge opened 30 June 1894 (by the Prince of Wales); built 1886–94 with ~11,000 tons of
  steel; its bascules raise to 86°; total cost £1,184,000. The high-level walkways are 42 m above
  the Thames; the glass floors were added in 2014. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Bridge ; https://www.towerbridge.org.uk/stories/timeline-the-construction-of-tower-bridge
- Borough Market traces its history to records of trading in Southwark in 1014 — about 1,000 years;
  the Borough Market Act of 1756 created its trustee governance, and its iron-and-glass buildings
  date to 1851. It's run as a charitable trust whose volunteer trustees must live locally.
  Sources: https://boroughmarket.org.uk/our-story/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_Market
- London Eye: 135 m (443 ft) tall, wheel diameter 120 m; opened to the public 9 March 2000; 32
  air-conditioned capsules (numbered 1–33, skipping unlucky 13), one for each London borough, each
  holding up to 25 people; one rotation ≈ 30 minutes. It was the world's tallest Ferris wheel until
  2006. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Eye

**Kid hooks**
- Standing on the Tower Bridge glass floor: 42 m of nothing between your sneakers and the Thames,
  with buses driving beneath you.
- The Eye skips capsule #13 for luck — count for yourselves.
- The original Victorian engines raised the bridge with steam power; the Engine Rooms are part of
  the ticket.
- Borough Market is older than the Tower of London — people were selling food here before William
  the Conqueror was born.

**Myths & mistakes**
- "That's London Bridge!" No — Tower Bridge. London Bridge is the plain one upstream (and the one
  in the nursery rhyme; a previous London Bridge was sold and rebuilt in Arizona in the 1960s
  *(widely documented)*).
- "The Eye is a Ferris wheel ride" — it's one slow, smooth 30-minute rotation; no thrills, all view.

**Practical insight**
- Mid-June sunset in London is ~9:20 PM *(approx.)* — very late for kids. Consider booking the Eye
  for golden-hour (~8 PM) rather than literal sunset, or simply ride in daylight. Borough Market's
  full market days are typically Wed–Sat; June 16 is a Tuesday — check current opening before
  promising it (https://boroughmarket.org.uk/). Walking route writes itself: Tower → Tower Bridge →
  riverside walk west → Borough Market (late lunch) → continue along the South Bank to the Eye.

### Stop 6 — Wed Jun 17: Ted Lasso Richmond, Notting Hill & Portobello Road, Kensington Palace

**Verified facts**
- Ted Lasso's pub, the fictional Crown & Anchor, is The Prince's Head, 28 The Green, Richmond —
  exteriors only (the interior is a studio set). Ted's flat doorway is on Paved Court (the show's
  "9¾-style" address isn't real). Richmond Green (~12 acres) features in Ted and Beard's commute.
  Sources: https://www.thetrainline.com/en-us/via/europe/uk/england/london/the-real-life-ted-lasso-filming-locations-you-visit-around-london ; https://www.visitbritain.com/en/things-to-do/visit-ted-lasso-filming-locations-britain
- Richmond Park: created as a hunting park by Charles I, who walled it (eight miles of brick) in
  1637; today 630+ red and fallow deer roam free. Source: https://www.royalparks.org.uk/visit/parks/richmond-park/history
- Kensington Palace: Queen Victoria was born here in 1819 and raised under the strict "Kensington
  System"; it was later home to Princess Diana (Apartments 8 & 9), and is the official London
  residence of the Prince and Princess of Wales. Open to visitors via Historic Royal Palaces.
  Sources: https://www.hrp.org.uk/kensington-palace/history-and-stories/the-story-of-kensington-palace/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensington_Palace
- Portobello Road Market: 19th-century origins; antiques dealers arrived in the late 1940s–50s, and
  it's now the UK's largest antiques market (~1,000 vendors). The road was named for Portobello
  Farm, itself named for the British naval victory at Porto Bello, Panama, in 1739. Backdrop of the
  1999 film *Notting Hill*. Sources: https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/portobello-market-londons-largest-antiques-market/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portobello_Road

**Kid hooks**
- Walk Ted's actual commute: Paved Court → Richmond Green → The Prince's Head. Recreate the
  "Believe" energy; the pub is a real, family-friendly-at-lunch local.
- Richmond Park deer descend from royal hunting herds — and during rutting/birthing seasons signs
  warn you to stay 50 m back. They are wild; do not feed.
- Victoria became queen at 18 after a childhood so controlled she reportedly couldn't walk
  downstairs without holding an adult's hand *(popular account of the Kensington System; details vary)*.

**Myths & mistakes**
- "We can drink in Ted's pub where they filmed the inside." Interiors were a studio recreation;
  the exterior and the pub itself are real, the rooms don't match the show exactly.
- "Richmond, Virginia / Richmond, London — same name, coincidence?" Fun family link: both trace
  back to Richmond upon Thames via Richmond Palace (Henry VII's), named for his Yorkshire earldom
  *(commonly given etymology; flag as background color, not a verified page fact)*.
- "Portobello is in full swing every day." Saturday is the famous full antiques day; June 17 is a
  Wednesday — stalls run but expect a quieter market, with shops and arcades open.

**Practical insight**
- This is a two-ends-of-London day: Richmond (southwest, District line) and Notting Hill/Kensington
  (west) pair fine, but don't add more. Order suggestion: Richmond morning (green, calm, deer),
  tube to Notting Hill for lunch/Portobello browse, finish at Kensington Palace & Gardens — the
  Diana Memorial Playground in Kensington Gardens (pirate ship) is a legendary kid payoff
  *(playground existence widely documented; check current entry rules)*.

### Stop 7 — Dinner at The Jugged Hare, 49 Chiswell Street (Barbican), Wed Jun 17

**Verified facts**
- The Jugged Hare occupies the corner of the Grade II-listed former Whitbread Brewery site.
  Samuel Whitbread founded the brewery in 1750 — the country's first purpose-built mass-production
  brewery — and beer was brewed on Chiswell Street until 1976.
  Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiswell_Street ; https://alondoninheritance.com/london-streets/chiswell-street-and-mr-whitbreads-brewery/
- The corner building served as the King's Head pub (recorded by 1796) — effectively the brewery
  tap. The current gastropub opened in 2012 under brothers Ed and Tom Martin (ETM Group),
  specializing in seasonal British game, spit-roast meats and wild fish.
  Sources: https://www.designmynight.com/london/pubs/city-of-london/the-jugged-hare ; http://www.londontown.com/LondonInformation/Dining/Jugged-Hare/5c0ff/
- The decor leans hard into the theme: taxidermy game birds and hares in windows and cases.
  Source: https://darkestlondon.com/tag/jugged-hare/

**Kid hooks**
- "Jugged hare" is a real (and really old) dish: hare slow-cooked in a tall jug, traditionally
  thickened with the animal's own blood — peak gross-out trivia to deploy at the table.
- The pub is decorated with actual taxidermy — send the kids on a count-the-creatures hunt.
- George III and Queen Charlotte visited Whitbread's brewery in 1787 — it was that famous
  *(widely recorded event; not independently re-verified here)*.

**Myths & mistakes**
- The cookbook quote "First, catch your hare," attributed to Hannah Glasse (or Mrs Beeton), is
  apocryphal — Glasse actually wrote "Take your hare when it is cased..." *(well-documented
  misquotation; good myth-bust for the page)*.
- "Game pub = nothing kids will eat." In practice it's a gastropub with roasts, fish and chips and
  burgers alongside the venison and grouse — but the menu is seasonal; check the current menu and
  confirm children are fine for your booking time (it's a City pub; early dinner ~6 PM is the
  family-friendly window). *(Current kids'-menu specifics not verified — call ahead.)*

**Practical insight**
- It's steps from the Barbican Centre — arrive early and let the kids gawk at the Barbican's
  brutalist towers and lakeside terrace. Game is the point: the adventurous order is venison or
  whatever's in season in June (game birds are mostly an autumn/winter thing — June menus lean to
  fish, lamb, and farmed game).

### Stop 8 — Full-day coach tour, Thu Jun 18: Stonehenge + Roman Baths (Bath)

**Verified facts — Stonehenge**
- First monument: circular earthwork enclosure c. 3000 BC; the central stone settings went up
  c. 2500 BC. Source: https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/history-and-stories/building-stonehenge/
- Sarsens average 25 tons (the Heel Stone ~30); 2020 research traced them to West Woods, ~16 miles
  away. The smaller bluestones (2–5 tons, up to ~80 of them) came from the Preseli Hills in
  south-west Wales — over 150 miles *(distance approx.; commonly cited 150–180 miles)*.
  Sources: https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/things-to-do/stone-circle/stones-of-stonehenge/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge

**Verified facts — Roman Baths**
- The temple to Sulis Minerva was built c. AD 60–70 at the hot spring the Celts already held
  sacred; the Romans called the town Aquae Sulis. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)
- The spring water rises at up to 46°C, ~240,000 gallons a day. Source: https://www.romanbaths.co.uk/fun-facts
- About 130 lead-alloy "curse tablets" were found in the Sacred Spring (excavated 1979/80) — written
  pleas asking the goddess to punish thieves; plus 12,000+ Roman coins thrown in as offerings, the
  largest votive coin deposit known from Britain. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_curse_tablets ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)
- Georgian Bath: the Royal Crescent (30 Grade I-listed houses, ~500 ft sweep) was built 1767–75 by
  John Wood the Younger; Bath was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 — the only UK
  city listed in its entirety. Jane Austen lived in Bath 1801–06 and set *Northanger Abbey* and
  *Persuasion* there (and privately disliked the place).
  Sources: https://visitbath.co.uk/things-to-do/the-royal-crescent-p56191 ; https://www.visitbath.co.uk/inspire-me/about-bath/history-and-heritage/unesco-world-heritage-site

**Kid hooks**
- Curse tablets = ancient Roman rage-texting: "someone stole my cloak at the baths — goddess,
  make them suffer." Kids can read translations in the museum.
- Stonehenge math: a 25-ton stone, no wheels, no metal tools, 5,000 years ago. How would *you*
  move it? (Ropes, sledges, log rollers, hundreds of people — archaeologists still debate.)
- The green, steaming water in the Great Bath is hot — but absolutely no touching (untreated water).
- The Romans soaked here like it was a 2,000-year-old water park: warm room, hot room, cold plunge.

**Myths & mistakes**
- "You can walk among the Stonehenge stones." Standard tickets keep you on a path around the circle;
  touching is not allowed. Inner-circle access exists only via special out-of-hours "Stone Circle
  Experience" bookings *(English Heritage offering; availability not verified for these dates)*.
- "Stonehenge was built by druids/Celts." It predates the Celts and druids by roughly two millennia.
- "You can swim in / drink from the Roman Baths." No — the original baths are look-only; the modern
  Thermae Bath Spa nearby is where people actually bathe *(separate commercial spa; not part of this tour)*.
- "Aliens." Sigh. No.

**Practical insight**
- Coach days run long (typically 10–11 hours); pack snacks, downloads, and layers — Salisbury Plain
  is windy even in June. At Stonehenge the audio guide/exhibition Neolithic houses are the kid-
  friendly bits; at Bath, the children's audio-guide track is genuinely good. If the tour allows
  free time in Bath, the 10-minute walk up to the Royal Crescent is the photo that says "Georgian
  England" — and ties the day back to Jane Austen for Laura.
