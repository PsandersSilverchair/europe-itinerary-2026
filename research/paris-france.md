## Regional arc

Every stop on the Sanders itinerary is really one story told eight ways: a river made a city, kings made it grand, a revolution made it everyone's, and engineers and artists made it modern.

**Start with the river.** Paris exists because of the Seine — the islands in the middle of it were defensible, and the river was the highway for everything else. That's why the family's very first activity, the boat cruise on arrival day, is the right overture: the historic banks of the Seine, from Pont de Sully to Pont d'Iéna, are a UNESCO World Heritage site precisely because the city's whole biography lines up along the water — Notre-Dame and Sainte-Chapelle for the Middle Ages, the Pont Neuf for the Renaissance, the Marais and Île Saint-Louis for the 17th–18th centuries (https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/600/). From the boat deck you can literally watch eight centuries scroll past in an hour.

**Then the fortress that became a palace that became a museum.** Around 1190, King Philip II (Philip Augustus) built the Louvre as a fortress guarding the river approach to Paris (https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/louvre-museum-robbery-mona-lisa). Kings kept remodeling it into a Renaissance palace — until Louis XIV got bored of it.

**The Sun King changes the channel.** In 1623 Louis XIII built a modest hunting lodge at Versailles; his son Louis XIV inflated it, in building campaigns from 1661 to 1715, into the largest political stage set in Europe, and in 1682 moved the entire court and government there (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Versailles; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Palace-of-Versailles). Versailles is absolutism in architecture: 357 mirrors in one hallway, fountains that performed on command, an estate of about 800 hectares. For Levi, the royal-history kid, this is the main event — and the place where royal history ends, too.

**Revolution flips the script.** In October 1789, Parisians marched on Versailles and brought the royal family back to Paris; within four years the monarchy was abolished, and in 1793 the Louvre — the king's old palace — opened to the public as the Muséum central des arts (https://www.npr.org/2025/10/20/nx-s1-5580221/louvre-museum-robbery-history). The thread for the kids: the same buildings keep standing, but who they belong to changes. Palaces become museums; the king's art becomes everybody's art. The Revolution also marks the timeline at the Eiffel Tower, built for the 1889 World's Fair celebrating the Revolution's centennial (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower).

**Haussmann bulldozes the medieval city — mostly.** Between 1853 and 1870, Napoleon III's prefect Georges-Eugène Haussmann demolished roughly 19,730 buildings and cut over 140 km of wide boulevards through the cramped medieval city, adding parks, sewers and the uniform cream-stone apartment blocks that define Paris today (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris). The boulevards set the stage for the belle époque — the optimistic decades around 1900 — which the family will taste directly at Bouillon Chartier (a workers' canteen from 1896 that never changed) and at the Musée d'Orsay, a gilded railway station from the 1900 World's Fair that now holds the Impressionists. The Marais matters because Haussmann largely *missed* it: it's the surviving pocket of pre-Haussmann Paris, with the city's oldest planned square (1612) and its historic Jewish quarter, the Pletzl.

**The iron age.** The 1889 fair's centerpiece, Gustave Eiffel's tower, went up in just 2 years, 2 months and 5 days and was meant to come down after 20 years — engineering as spectacle, and the world's tallest structure until 1929 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower). The same era's confidence built the Gare d'Orsay (1900), the world's first electrified urban terminal station (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gare_d%27Orsay).

**And the hill that watched it all.** Montmartre, the butte above the city, was cheap, semi-rural and full of windmills — so the painters moved in. Renoir painted the *Bal du moulin de la Galette* there in 1876 (the family will see it at the Orsay) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bal_du_moulin_de_la_Galette). Looming over the artists' village is Sacré-Cœur, begun 1875 as a "national vow" after France's defeat by Prussia in 1870–71 — a church whose political meaning Parisians have argued about ever since (https://www.rte.ie/brainstorm/2025/0919/1534257-sacre-coeur-basilica-paris-montmartre-history/).

**Recurring themes for the pages:**
1. **The river built the city** — every stop is within sight of, or exists because of, the Seine.
2. **Buildings that changed jobs** — fortress→palace→museum (Louvre), station→museum (Orsay), royal stage→public monument (Versailles), "temporary" fair exhibit→eternal icon (Eiffel).
3. **Who owns Paris?** — kings, then revolutionaries, then everyone: the long handover of palaces, art and boulevards to the public.
4. **Old Paris vs. new Paris** — Haussmann's boulevards vs. the Marais's crooked survivor streets; Montmartre's village vs. the city below.

**Etiquette / français cheat sheet (use everywhere):** In France you *always* open with "Bonjour" (or "Bonsoir" after ~6 pm) before asking anything — walking into a shop or up to a ticket window without it reads as rude. Kit for the whole family: *Bonjour / Bonsoir* (hello), *Merci* (thank you), *S'il vous plaît* (please), *Parlez-vous anglais ?* (do you speak English?), *L'addition, s'il vous plaît* (the check, please). A kid who delivers a confident "Bonjour!" will be treated like visiting royalty.

## Per-stop notes

### Stop 1 — Bateaux Parisiens Seine cruise (Eiffel Tower dock), afternoon Jun 19

**Verified facts**
- 1-hour round-trip cruise departing from Port de la Bourdonnais at the foot of the Eiffel Tower; individual audio guides in 14 languages; departures roughly every 30 minutes, ~10:00–22:00 in high season (https://www.bateauxparisiens.com/english.html; https://parisjetaime.com/eng/tickets/sightseeing-cruise-bateaux-parisiens-m9000734; https://www.getyourguide.com/paris-l16/paris-1-hour-seine-cruise-departing-from-the-eiffel-tower-t193940/).
- Route passes the Eiffel Tower, Invalides, Musée d'Orsay, Conciergerie, Notre-Dame, Hôtel de Ville, the Louvre and the Grand Palais (https://www.viator.com/tours/Paris/Bateaux-Parisiens-Seine-River-Sightseeing-Cruise/d479-5756SIGHTSEEING).
- The banks of the Seine in central Paris (Pont de Sully to Pont d'Iéna, plus both islands) are a UNESCO World Heritage site (https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/600/).
- The Pont Neuf — "New Bridge" — is actually Paris's *oldest* surviving bridge, completed in 1607 under Henry IV, and the first built without houses on it (https://delveintoeurope.com/pont-neuf-paris/; https://www.cometoparis.com/paris-guide/paris-monuments/pont-neuf-s960).

**Kid hooks**
- Bridge-counting contest: the boat slips under bridge after bridge — have Levi and Micah keep score and spot the gold statues on Pont Alexandre III.
- The "New Bridge" is the oldest bridge — a 400-year-old naming prank the whole family can groan at.
- Spot the mini Statue of Liberty: there's a small Lady Liberty on an island downstream — same sculptor connection as New York's (point it out if the route allows). **[NOT WEB-VERIFIED detail]**
- Wave at other boats — Parisians on the quays genuinely wave back at cruise boats.

**Myths / mistakes**
- Bateaux Parisiens ≠ Bateaux-Mouches: different companies, different docks. Theirs leaves from the Eiffel Tower side — don't follow signs to the Pont de l'Alma dock.
- "The audio guide is skippable" — for first-timers who don't know the skyline, it's the difference between a boat ride and a history lesson.

**Practical**
- Perfect jet-lag activity: seated, outdoors, one hour. Top deck for views; June afternoon sun can be strong — sit on the shaded side or bring hats.
- Arrive ~20 min early; boarding is pontoon no. 3 at Port de la Bourdonnais (https://www.viator.com/tours/Paris/Bateaux-Parisiens-Seine-River-Sightseeing-Cruise/d479-5756SIGHTSEEING).
- The Eiffel Tower esplanade nearby is prime territory for the petition and three-cup-trick scams — politely ignore clipboard-carriers (https://www.corporatetravelsafety.com/safety-tips/fake-petition-scam-in-paris-street-thieves-target-tourists-on-the-street/).

### Stop 2 — Château de Versailles (Passeport, 11:00 AM Jun 20, Entrance A)

**Verified facts**
- Louis XIII built a hunting lodge here in 1623; Louis XIV expanded it in phases 1661–1715 and moved the court and government to Versailles in 1682 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Versailles; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Palace-of-Versailles).
- Hall of Mirrors: 73 m long, 357 mirrors, built from 1678 under Jules Hardouin-Mansart (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_of_Mirrors). The Treaty of Versailles ending WWI was signed there in 1919. **[treaty location: NOT WEB-VERIFIED in this session's results, standard history]**
- Estate today: about 800 hectares of gardens and parkland (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Versailles).
- October 1789: the Women's March on Versailles forced Louis XVI and the royal family back to Paris — the palace's days as seat of power ended (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Versailles).
- Musical Fountains Show 2026: every Saturday and Sunday, Apr 4–Nov 1, plus select Tuesdays May–June; the Passeport includes timed palace entry, the Gardens *including fountain-show days*, the Trianon estate and the Park (https://en.chateauversailles.fr/news/shows/musical-fountains-show-and-musical-gardens; https://www.chateauversailles-spectacles.fr/en/evenement/the-musical-fountains-show-2026/). **Jun 20, 2026 is a Saturday — the fountains should be running and their Passeport covers it.**

**Kid hooks**
- The fountains are baroque special effects: hidden waterworks "dance" to music written for Louis XIV. In the king's day there wasn't enough water to run them all at once — fountaineers whistled signals to switch fountains on just before the king walked by. **[whistling-signals anecdote: NOT WEB-VERIFIED, popular but commonly told by Versailles guides]**
- 357 mirrors: in the 1680s mirrors were luxury tech (Venice guarded the secret), so a hallway of them was like paving a corridor with flatscreens.
- Louis XIV chose the sun as his personal logo — have the kids count sun faces on gates, fountains and ceilings.
- The palace started as a teenager's hunting cabin — then his son turned it into the biggest flex in Europe.

**Myths / mistakes**
- "Let them eat cake": there's no evidence Marie-Antoinette ever said it — the line predates her. Flag as legend on the page.
- Don't try to "do it all": Palace + Gardens + Trianon + Hamlet is a full marathon. With kids: palace first (your 11:00 slot), picnic/garden afternoon, Marie-Antoinette's toy-village Hamlet if energy allows.

**Practical**
- Arrive at Entrance A ~15–30 min before the 11:00 slot; security lines build by mid-morning. RER C from central Paris to Versailles Château Rive Gauche is the standard route.
- Fountain shows run in timed sessions — grab the day's schedule on arrival and plan the Latona/Apollo basins around it.
- Rent bikes or the little train for the gardens if legs flag; the park is enormous (≈800 ha).
- Eat before or picnic: on-site food is pricey with long lines.

### Stop 3 — Le Marais dinner: Rue des Rosiers & around, evening Jun 20

**Verified facts**
- The Pletzl ("little place" in Yiddish) around Rue des Rosiers is the historic heart of Jewish Paris: a Jewish presence from the 13th century, re-established after the 1791 Emancipation, with waves of Alsatian Jews (early 1800s) and Eastern European Jews fleeing persecution (from the 1880s) (https://www.mahj.org/en/visit/jewish-marais; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pletzl).
- L'As du Fallafel, 34 Rue des Rosiers — kosher, famous falafel-eggplant-hummus sandwich; queues regularly stretch down the street (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%27As_du_Fallafel; https://parisbymouth.com/las-du-falafel/).
- Place des Vosges, built 1605–1612 under Henry IV and inaugurated in 1612 for the engagement festivities of Louis XIII and Anne of Austria, is the oldest planned square in Paris (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_des_Vosges).
- In 1962 culture minister André Malraux placed a protection order on the Marais, saving the old quarter from redevelopment (https://bonjourparis.com/archives/marais-jewish-history-pletzl-changes-paris-4th/).

**Kid hooks**
- Royal-history bonus for Levi: Place des Vosges opened with a party for a 11-year-old king's engagement, and Victor Hugo (the *Hunchback of Notre-Dame* author) later lived at No. 6 — now a free museum. **[Hugo at No. 6: NOT WEB-VERIFIED in this session, well-established]**
- A falafel pita the size of your head, eaten standing in a medieval street — best cheap dinner in Paris.
- The Marais escaped Haussmann's bulldozers — it's the "before" photo of Paris. Crooked streets = older streets.

**Myths / mistakes**
- L'As du Fallafel is closed Friday evening/Saturday for Shabbat — fine for the Sanders' Saturday-*evening* plan only if confirmed; **check before going on Jun 20 (a Saturday)** — they typically reopen after sundown or Sunday. Backup falafel windows line the same street, and classic bistros sit a few blocks away.
- "The Marais is just boutiques now" — partly true (gentrification has thinned the old kosher shops — https://bonjourparis.com/archives/marais-jewish-history-pletzl-changes-paris-4th/), but synagogues, bakeries and the Shoah Memorial keep the quarter's memory present. Treat it as living history, not a theme set.

**Practical**
- Takeaway window moves much faster than table service; eat in Place des Vosges' arcades or on the square's lawns.
- Evening is lovely here: the Marais keeps later hours than much of Paris, and it's an easy walk or one-stop Métro back to Étienne Marcel.

### Stop 4 — Eiffel Tower guided tour, 2nd floor by lift, 5:30 PM Jun 21

**Verified facts**
- Built 1887–1889 by Gustave Eiffel's company as the centerpiece of the 1889 Exposition Universelle marking the French Revolution's centennial; construction took 2 years, 2 months and 5 days (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower; https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/the-monument/history).
- 330 m tall (with antennas); world's tallest structure until New York's Chrysler Building topped out in 1929 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower).
- Meant to be temporary: the permit ran 20 years, with dismantling slated for 1909 — it was saved by its usefulness for radio telegraphy (https://www.history.com/articles/eiffel-tower-survival).
- On Feb 14, 1887, ~300 artists and writers (Maupassant, Charles Garnier, Dumas fils among them) published a protest in *Le Temps* against the "useless and monstrous" tower (https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/130-years/artists-who-protested-eiffel-tower).
- About 18,000 individually calculated iron pieces; repainted roughly every 7 years — about 20 repaints so far (https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/the-monument/painting-eiffel-tower).

**Kid hooks**
- People *hated* it. France's most famous monument got one-star reviews from 300 celebrities before it was even finished — great lesson in "wait and see."
- It was supposed to be scrap metal by 1909. Radio saved it: the antenna made the tower too useful to tear down.
- Legend says Maupassant ate lunch in the tower's restaurant because it was the only place in Paris he couldn't see the tower — almost certainly embellished, but too good not to tell (flag as legend).
- It's repainted by hand, by climbers with brushes, every ~7 years.

**Myths / mistakes**
- "Photographing the Eiffel Tower at night is illegal" — the night lighting is copyrighted, but that targets commercial use; family photos are not a problem. Worth debunking on the page.
- Booking "2nd floor" is not settling: many guides argue the 2nd floor has the *better* view (you can still see Paris's details; the summit is higher but hazier). Manage expectations so nobody feels short-changed.

**Practical**
- 5:30 PM in late June = full daylight (Paris sunset is ~9:55 PM); for sparkle photos, the tower glitters for 5 minutes at the top of each hour after dark — visible from their cruise dock area or Champ de Mars another evening.
- Security screening before the lifts; pack light. Arrive 30 min early for a timed guided tour.
- Scam alert around Trocadéro/Champ de Mars: bracelet weavers, petition clipboards, three-cup games (https://nomavue.com/paris-tourist-scams/). Firm "Non, merci" and keep walking; keep phones zipped.

### Stop 5 — Jun 21 ideas: Musée d'Orsay + dinner at Bouillon Chartier

**Verified facts**
- The Gare d'Orsay opened 28 May 1900 (architect Victor Laloux) for the 1900 Exposition Universelle — the world's first electrified urban terminal station (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gare_d%27Orsay).
- Reopened as the Musée d'Orsay in December 1986, interior by Gae Aulenti; the giant station clocks survive (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_d%27Orsay; https://www.france.fr/en/article/orsay-train-station-museum/).
- Holds the world's largest collection of Impressionist and post-Impressionist art — Monet, Renoir, Degas, Cézanne, Van Gogh, Gauguin (https://www.france.fr/en/article/orsay-museum/).
- Renoir's *Bal du moulin de la Galette* (1876), painted in Montmartre, hangs here (https://www.musee-orsay.fr/en/artworks/bal-du-moulin-de-la-galette-497) — see it the day before the family walks the actual Montmartre streets it depicts.
- Bouillon Chartier: opened 1896 by brothers Frédéric and Camille Chartier; classified monument historique in 1989; waiters in black waistcoat and long apron still tot up the bill on the paper tablecloth (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouillon_Chartier; https://www.bouillon-chartier.com/en/our-story/).

**Kid hooks**
- Stand behind the giant glass clock face on the upper level — you're *inside* a clock, looking out over Paris (Hugo Cabret vibes).
- The museum is a train station: platforms became galleries; imagine steam engines where the sculptures now sit.
- At Chartier, the waiter does the math on your tablecloth — and dinner can cost less than the Eiffel Tower gift shop.

**Myths / mistakes**
- "The Mona Lisa is at the Orsay" — no; Orsay is 1848–1914 art. Leonardo lives at the Louvre (tomorrow).
- Chartier takes no reservations; the queue moves fast but exists. Sharing tables with strangers is part of the fun, not a mistake.

**Practical**
- Sunday Jun 21: book Orsay tickets online; the 5th-floor Impressionist gallery is the must-do with kids — aim for ~6–8 paintings, not the whole museum (tower tour is at 5:30 PM, so plan Orsay for late morning/early afternoon and keep it to ~2 hours).
- Under-18s are free at the Orsay. **[NOT WEB-VERIFIED this session — confirm on musee-orsay.fr]**
- Chartier Grands Boulevards is a ~20-minute walk from the apartment; go before 7 PM or expect a line.

### Stop 6 — Montmartre & Sacré-Cœur morning wander, Jun 22

**Verified facts**
- Sacré-Cœur: begun 1875, completed 1914, consecrated 1919 after WWI; Paul Abadie's Romano-Byzantine design won over 77 competing proposals (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacr%C3%A9-C%C5%93ur,_Paris).
- Built of Château-Landon travertine (quarried near Souppes-sur-Loing) that bleaches whiter with rain and age (https://www.rte.ie/brainstorm/2025/0919/1534257-sacre-coeur-basilica-paris-montmartre-history/).
- The "Savoyarde" bell weighs about 19 tonnes — among the largest in France (https://www.parisperfect.com/plan-your-trip/things-to-see/monuments-landmarks/sacre-coeur.php).
- Conceived as a "national vow" after France's 1870–71 defeat by Prussia; its relationship to the crushed Paris Commune of 1871 (which began on Montmartre) remains debated — some see it as penance imposed on a rebel neighborhood, defenders dispute that framing. Present it as a genuinely contested history (https://www.rte.ie/brainstorm/2025/0919/1534257-sacre-coeur-basilica-paris-montmartre-history/).
- Renoir painted *Bal du moulin de la Galette* here in 1876, working from a studio on Rue Cortot (now part of the Musée de Montmartre) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bal_du_moulin_de_la_Galette).

**Kid hooks**
- The church is self-cleaning: its special stone turns whiter every time it rains.
- A 19-tonne bell — roughly three African elephants — swings in the tower.
- Yesterday's painting, today's street: stand where Renoir's dancers partied at the Moulin de la Galette (the windmill still exists, now a restaurant).
- The funicular up the butte is part of the Métro system — a ride counts as a regular ticket. **[NOT WEB-VERIFIED this session]**

**Myths / mistakes**
- Sacré-Cœur looks ancient but is younger than the Eiffel Tower — finished in 1914, not the Middle Ages.
- Place du Tertre portrait artists: charming, but agree the price *before* anyone starts sketching the kids.

**Practical**
- Morning is the right call: crowds and hawkers multiply after ~11 AM. Basilica entry is free; the dome climb costs extra and is 300 steps. **[dome step count: NOT WEB-VERIFIED]**
- The bracelet scam operates on the staircases below the basilica — scammers tie a "friendship bracelet" on a wrist, then demand money. Keep hands in pockets on the stairs, say a firm "Non," or take the funicular and skip the gauntlet entirely (https://www.corporatetravelsafety.com/safety-tips/infamous-paris-string-scam/; https://www.travelandtourworld.com/news/article/paris-travel-warning-as-visitors-to-sacre-coeur-in-montmartre-targeted-by-friendship-bracelet-scam-with-tourist-numbers-reaching-10-5-million/).
- Combine: Place du Tertre → Rue Cortot/Musée de Montmartre → vineyard lane → basilica terrace view, then Métro toward the Louvre for the 3:30 slot.

### Stop 7 — The Louvre, 3:30 PM Jun 22 (Pyramid/Carrousel entrance)

**Verified facts**
- Began ~1190 as Philip II Augustus's fortress guarding Paris's river approach; later a royal palace (https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/louvre-museum-robbery-mona-lisa).
- Opened to the public in 1793, during the Revolution, as the Muséum central des arts (https://www.npr.org/2025/10/20/nx-s1-5580221/louvre-museum-robbery-history).
- I. M. Pei's glass Pyramid is the main entrance, opened 1989 (https://www.paristickets.com/louvre-museum/about/).
- World's most-visited museum: 8.7 million visitors in 2024 (https://presse.louvre.fr/?p=1063000223874).
- The Mona Lisa (oil on poplar, 77 × 53 cm — smaller than most people expect) was stolen on 21 August 1911 by Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian decorator who had worked at the museum; recovered in Florence over two years later. The theft made it the world's most famous painting (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Peruggia; https://www.washingtonpost.com/history/2019/10/20/how-theft-mona-lisa-made-it-worlds-most-famous-painting/).

**Kid hooks**
- The Mona Lisa was once rolled out the door under a workman's smock — and for two years people lined up to stare at the empty hook.
- In the basement of the Sully wing you can walk around the actual moat and towers of the 1190s castle — a fortress hiding under the museum. **[medieval-Louvre exhibit: NOT WEB-VERIFIED this session, long-standing permanent display]**
- Sticker-album challenge: hunt a shortlist of works instead of "seeing the Louvre."
- For Levi: this was the kings' palace *before* Versailles — Louis XIV grew up here.

**Myths / mistakes**
- "We'll see everything" — impossible and miserable; the collection and palace are vast. With kids, two hours and 8–10 works is a triumph.
- The Mona Lisa room is a scrum; see her first or skip-and-circle-back, and warn the kids she's small and behind glass.

**Practical — a kid-sized 8-to-10-work circuit (mostly Denon wing + Sully):**
1. Medieval Louvre moats (Sully, lower ground) 2. Venus de Milo 3. Winged Victory of Samothrace (top of the grand staircase) 4. Mona Lisa 5. Veronese's *Wedding at Cana* (the giant painting facing her) 6. Delacroix, *Liberty Leading the People* 7. Géricault, *Raft of the Medusa* 8. The Great Sphinx of Tanis (Egyptian rooms) 9. Code of Hammurabi 10. Napoleon III Apartments (Richelieu, if stamina allows).
- A 3:30 PM entry is smart — crowds thin late in the day. Confirm evening hours for Monday Jun 22 in advance on louvre.fr; under-18s enter free. **[free-under-18 and day-of hours: NOT WEB-VERIFIED this session]**
- The Carrousel du Louvre (underground mall) entrance usually has shorter security lines than the Pyramid itself.

### Stop 8 — Farewell dinner near Les Halles / 2nd arr., evening Jun 22

**Verified facts**
- Les Halles was Paris's central food market for centuries — Zola's "belly of Paris" — and the surrounding bistros grew up feeding its workers; the market itself moved out (to Rungis) in 1969–71. **[move date: NOT WEB-VERIFIED this session; market's existence and bistro link verified below]**
- **Au Pied de Cochon** (6 Rue Coquillière, 1st arr., ~4 min walk from the apartment): opened 1947 to feed Les Halles market workers and shoppers, famously ran 24/7 for decades; signature French onion soup — 150–200 bowls a day (https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/au-pied-de-cochon-paris; https://www.eutouring.com/au_pied_de_cochon_history.html).
- **La Poule au Pot** (Rue Vauvilliers, Les Halles): classic bistro founded 1935, décor barely changed for decades, long a late-night institution; named for Henry IV's promised "a chicken in every pot" dish (https://francerevisited.com/2017/08/paris-night-bistro-la-poule-au-pot/).

**Kid hooks**
- Au Pied de Cochon means "At the Pig's Foot" — dare the table to order it (there's plenty of chicken, steak-frites and onion soup for the cautious).
- These bistros once served butchers and vegetable-haulers at 4 AM; the onion soup was the market workers' (and night owls') restorative.
- *La poule au pot* is literally King Henry IV's campaign promise — royal history on a plate for Levi: the same king who built the Pont Neuf and Place des Vosges they've already seen.

**Myths / mistakes**
- Skip the tourist-trap cafés ringing the Westfield Forum des Halles mall; the real classics are on the quieter streets just west and north.
- French dinner service starts ~7 PM; showing up at 5:30 with hungry kids gets you an empty room or a closed door — bridge the gap with a goûter (pastry snack) around 4–5 PM.

**Practical**
- Both are a 5-minute walk from 38 Rue Étienne Marcel. Book ahead for ~7:00–7:30 PM (Au Pied de Cochon takes online reservations).
- Bonus 30 seconds from the apartment: the Tour Jean-sans-Peur on Rue Étienne Marcel, a genuine medieval tower surviving among the shops — a fitting last look at "old Paris." **[NOT WEB-VERIFIED this session]**
- Toast the trip with the cheat sheet: "L'addition, s'il vous plaît" — then one last walk to the Seine (10 min) for the Eiffel Tower's hourly sparkle.
